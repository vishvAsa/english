## Siddhartha
They say that after Siddhartha left home, he learned some philosophies, tried some yogas, and conducted experiments of his own. Nothing seemed to work. One evening, he decided to wait. Just wait, and do nothing. Aśvaghoṣa captures this moment in the words: 

> “I shall not break this seat upon the earth; I shall not depart until I have accomplished my purpose.” 

At that moment, Siddhartha transcends our time-bound life of one chore after another: I will do this, then that; I will save some time for X, and then I will do Y. He enters a different relation with time, one in which he no longer hurries to make things happen. That night, Siddhartha awakens to the realization that grants him enlightenment.

## Learning to wait
It took me many years even to scratch the surface of waiting. I always thought myself intelligent, perhaps more so than my colleagues, and complained to Guruji about making me wait alongside “them,” the lazy ones. I later discovered that I had to learn to wait actively, allowing understanding to unfold of its own accord. 

There are so many things I wish I knew. Yet I know that I must also be at peace with the possibility that I may not be meant to realize them in this cycle of being. For, on many occasions, what I have been searching for appears the very next day after I promise myself to stop looking for it.

There are things I have seen as if in dreams. Later, I feel I must let go of my obsession with pursuing them, for they may never have been real. And once I bid farewell to the things inhabiting the realm of my mind, they appear in full form the next day. Or eventually.

And some never appear. It is not that I give up and they simply come. Some quests never find their way into manifestation; some desires are never actualized. 

Sometimes I wait; at other times, I forget that I have been waiting. It seems there is another realm of realization, with a mind of its own, a life of its own, that does not follow my anguish or my zeal. It simply is. It comes like the dawn, in its own time, according to its own cycle.

## Dawn
And yet we wait. And yet we pray. Does it make a difference if we actively call for the dawn? A Ṛṣi sings: “This light has come, the finest of lights; wondrous, far-spreading radiance has been born. Night, as though giving birth, has made room for Dawn.” Would the dawn not have come had the Ṛṣi not offered his prayers?

I believe there is something personal about the dawn. It comes as a simmering light for one, while another does not see it at all. I remember waking in the middle of the night, jumping into a jeep, and riding for several hours to a viewpoint above a vast volcanic landscape, where I watched the dawn reveal Mount Bromo in Indonesia. The mountains are the same; the chilly dawns are the same; the mists are the same. It is only the moment of epiphany, the moment of camatkāra, the moment of realization, that is never quite the same.

You can be smart and save thousands by sleeping in the comfort of your own bed, or you can travel across the world to catch a glimpse of the sunrise.

Whether you wait or not, it makes no difference to the sun. The dawns are the same, whether or not you have waited for her. It is only that waiting makes a difference to you.+++(4)+++

## Conclusion
Siddhartha did not awaken to a new reality. He awakened with clarity. He had allowed time to pass, relinquishing his need to command it, and surrendered his ego to a vow that would hold him there, even unto eternity.
