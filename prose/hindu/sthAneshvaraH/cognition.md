## Metacognition in animals
Do animals and birds have meta-cognition? We can all agree that animals and birds are conscious, are capable of cognizing, but how can you evaluate your own mental states and repeat the acts that you think are causal in producing the mental state of pleasure in absence of metacognition? Playing for fun is not uncommon among animals or birds. But these also demonstrate mimicry, particularly copying human actions. If they are able to copy human actions and are able to evaluate their mental states, are they also able to evaluate the mental states of the humans that they are copying? And if so, they are also inter-subjectively aware. For me, these are not problems, because the way I understand consciousness is it can never be bereft of reflexivity and inter-subjectivity.

## Active Forgetting
Sometimes we wish we could switch our brain off and forget some of the events that we underwent or we witnessed. While there may not be agentive or active forgetting, this is fascinating to learn how our brain has the mechanism to push some of our memories to the oblivion. Some meditation techniques might reveal how active forgetting is not only possible but also worth exploring. This paper ("Brain may actively forget during dream sleep") at least explains the neurology of forgetting.

## In the age of information
Just like they used to strive for information, we today need to consciously choose to NOT know. It is different than forgetting. We do not need well-informed teachers any more, as all the information is out there; we need teachers who can help us liberate from what we know. I once read "ignorance is bondage" (ajñānaṃ bandhaḥ). Now I have surrendered to reading it as "knowledge is bondage" (jñānaṃ bandhaḥ). 

We have to make a conscious decision every moment to ignore, to close our eyes and ears, and close the gates of the mind and the heart. What we know hurts more today than our ignorance. There is so much that we know but means nothing as we are wrapped around information and handicapped by the overdose of information. I am not even talking about false information. They may all be true information but they hurt our active participation just the same.

## Suffering and Amnesia
I have long wanted to write about forgetting. The first time I resolved to do so, I was even given a deadline for an essay on forgetting—and I forgot it. Completely. There could scarcely have been a more fitting beginning. The editor, with uncommon grace, waited patiently for my manuscript.

Perhaps none of us has awakened like Siddhartha, yet all of us know something of suffering and its quiet conspiracy with forgetfulness. We suffer, and we forget. We must. Otherwise pain would accumulate without mercy, layer upon layer, until memory itself became unbearable.

Cultures learn to carry suffering in different ways. In theistic traditions, suffering often draws close to the divine and, in doing so, becomes strangely ordinary. Consolation arrives in a simple thought: if even God suffers, who am I to expect exemption?

Among the stories that have stayed with me, none moves me more than that of Nilakantha—the Blue-Throated One.

When gods and demons churned the primordial ocean in search of immortality, they did so together, each driven by desire. The treasures emerged one by one. Indra claimed his celestial elephant. Vishnu received Lakshmi and the radiant jewels. Every gift found its owner. Yet before the nectar of immortality could be gathered, something older and darker surfaced from the depths: a poison capable of undoing the worlds.

No god stepped forward to claim it.
Only then did they remember Shiva.
He drank the poison without hesitation. It lodged in his throat, staining it forever blue, and thus he became Nilakantha. In my imagination, the poison did more than change the color of his neck. It transformed him inwardly, alchemically. Perhaps he already sensed that he was becoming a stranger in the southern lands, where the austere order of the Tamil Brahmins was steadily eclipsing the wild ascetic. So he wandered north, toward the Himalayas.

One day he crossed the Betravati and climbed Lauribina. The poison still burned within him, an unending fire coursing through his body. Unable to bear its heat, he drove his trident into the earth. Three springs burst forth, gathering into the glacial waters of Gosai Kunda.

My grandmother once told me that when she made the pilgrimage there, she saw Shiva asleep beneath the stillness of the lake.
I have never forgotten that image.

The other story that continues to haunt me is Krishna's brief amnesia.

Moments after revealing to Arjuna the deepest mysteries of existence, the war begins. Under Bhisma's command, the Kaurava army surges with terrible force. Bhisma cannot be stopped. At last Krishna, overcome with fury, casts aside the reins, lifts a broken chariot wheel, and strides across the battlefield, determined to strike him down.

For a fleeting instant, he forgets.
He forgets that he is God. He forgets the vow he made not to wield a weapon. He forgets the infinite stillness from which his own actions arise.

And in that vast battlefield, perhaps in the whole world, only Bhisma remembers.

Recognizing the Divine before him, Bhisma lowers his weapons and offers himself without resistance. He knows he has no quarrel with the Absolute. There is nothing left to defend.

Perhaps that is the strange mercy hidden within these stories.  
Even the gods bear poison. Even the gods forget.  

Their suffering is not merely a spectacle for human consolation, nor their forgetfulness a flaw in perfection. They remind us that suffering is something carried across worlds, and forgetting is not always a failure of memory but sometimes its deepest compassion. Without forgetting, wounds would never scar over; without suffering, compassion would never ripen.

Sometimes we need only remember this: that suffering and forgetfulness are not ours alone to carry.
