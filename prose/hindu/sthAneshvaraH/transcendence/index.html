Shorts

Every society or civilization is founded on myth. It is not the power of truth but of myth that keeps the culture alive. The first liberation is to free oneself from these constructions.

Freedom

It is the inner drive, a surge of being that overpowers the conditions of us being in whichever states. This is not a judgment upon something, this is not a philosophy or politics. We do not explore freedom, it is the other way around. It is the freedom that has found us and freedom will keep finding all the new forms.

Potter

No matter how good the potter, he still needs good clay for pottery. No matter how good the clay, he still needs to beat it and hammer it till it is ready. And finally, when the pottery is made, nobody admires the clay but says, what an amazing potter.

Only when you are ready to die, seek for what you can become. And when you are reborn, it will not be a newer version of yourself. An oak tree is not a newer version of an acorn.

Old man and houses

Old man loved building houses. He hated the engineers and he taught the woodworkers and the stoneworkers. He did not like others telling him how to build a house. He built houses five times more than Milarespa did. For every time, he saw imperfection in his creation. He was his own Marpa. And he invoked Kali in between these constructions. Sometimes he summoned her while the construction was ongoing, but mostly it was in between.

And the realization dawned. The perfect house, he said, does not need a roof. And he took out the roof. And he took out the windows and the doors. For he saw no point of the doors. And he broke down the walls, for why would a good house need walls. And finally old man sat in samadhi in his perfect house. He soaked his body with scorching heat and drenched himself in the rain. Neither did the cold of the winter deter him. And he took his samadhi when all alone. Old man had come to the realization that he was the foundation for both form and emptiness.

The Tree

It was a tree till the day they decided to cut it and turn it into wood. They cut it further and sold it in the market: after all, it was a precious wood. I did get a piece of it, not as wood but a statue of Narasimha, Man-lion, a reverse Sphinx. I do not know what happened to the rest of it but a part was turned into a decorated window and another into sarangi to sing folklore embedded with history.

A ritualist came and installed life in the statue and ever since, the statue became a deity. They installed the window for people to admire for the artwork and it became a museum piece. A good musician purchased the sarangi and the instrument, they say, sings alone the rhythms of the cosmos.

It was a middle aged tree with a couple hollow branches. It just was and had no meaning when as a tree. Being turned into wood was not the discovery of meaning either. It was nonetheless necessary. After all, the wood-worker could not have made so many objects while as a tree.

They say trees do not feel pain. We will have to ask this to the trees. But the meaning the tree discovered, the new becomings that unfolded the meaning of being for the tree is not for the tree itself. No meaning has ever been for itself. There always is a marketplace for the meaning to be discovered. Every time a saw runs through the chest of a tree, there is meaning waiting to unfurl. When the tree stops being a tree, stops having meaning for itself, abnegates its subjectivity and becomes a piece of wood, it becomes meaningful for so many. After all, I love the statue of Narasimha.

Real Yogi

Source: TW

I must have been 18. Haridwar is quite a place for young seekers. It was a Kumbh Mela to begin with. Everybody knew what they were there for. I was there to realize myself. I did not know at that time that realization is a gradual process, and we can only realize a layer at a time.

My first realization came in the Ganges when I was bathing. It just so happens that the princess of one Indian dynasty happened to dip into the Ganges at the same time. Her servants were not letting her go, but finally she freed herself, so for a minute she could be on her own, touching the waters of the Mother Ganges. I was reciting mantras, admiring the beauty of the princess, and also immersing into water. But most importantly, I was watching my own mind to see if I was having any carnal thoughts. For it would be hypocritical for a Brahmacharin to have desire. And also, if I had no desire to be next to the princess, I would have no reason to worry about myself.

As if some divine plan were unfolding or the bulb of some esoteric wisdom shining, as soon as the princess dipped into the Ganges and surfaced, she was completely undraped. There was nothing between the princess and me, and her Sari started floating on the waters like some lilies floating on a lake. Everything was happening so fast, I did not know what I was processing and whatnot.

One thing that shocked me was that, rather than approaching her dress, she started screaming. And two of her servants came rushing. They were able to catch the dress on time and drape her back. The princess was hysterically mad, and she beat both the servants. I still do not know why.

This was my first time dipping into Ganges. Nobody can convince me that bathing in Ganges is meaningless.

I thought, " This is too much. I need a quiet time for myself to meditate." And everywhere was a crowd. I remember finding a hillside with some forest. Fortunately, it was outside the attraction, and no crowd was walking through the forest. I entered and kept going as long as I did not feel completely alone. I sat there, perfectly happy in my own spot of meditation. I was finally freeing myself from the noise, outside and within.

To my surprise, I heard some people walking by. And the sound approached closer and closer. And I could hear one man saying to the other, “Look, this is what a real Sadhu looks like. He is simply avoiding the crowd. Let us give him some money.” And they dropped some bills next to my seat and left.

I was now annoyed. I saw no point in sitting and meditating there. I already achieved the title a Sadhu can ever hope for. I did not know what to do with that money. I just picked it up and prayed to Lord Shiva, may the first Sadhu I see be a good one. And gave it all without trying to find out what was there.

I do believe that these moments were as close as they can get in terms of self-discovery.

Revelation

Revelation

There is the other me. Not the me that I know, but the me they know. The other me is elusive to me, even though it is always comfortable to greet others.

I had noticed his presence from his shadows, but the first time I encountered him was when I left home. To tell you the truth, I did not leave home; the home left me. I saw the other me. I realized myself a total stranger in the looks of those who were integral to me. Consciousness must be nondual to start with. It is the other me that fragments consciousness. The other was more pronounced when I was in town. “A village boy with dreams.”

It takes courage to be the other. To embrace the other. I learnt to be stubborn. It was not courage but stubbornness. For I did not have just the courage to be the other anymore. I wanted to be the radical other. This other me helped me realize myself more than the seeker me. The other me helped me find my company among the monks from Shamkara’s order. This helped me befriend some Aghoris. Fortunately, let me grow under the shadow of some loving human beings, such as Prem Chetan or Khaptad Baba. I gave up the idea of being me. I allowed the other to take over.

I do not know how seeking nonduality can ever meet with embracing radical difference. All I can say is that it became organic to my being. Being among different clubs and savoring the difference became integral to my self-experience. But very little I knew, this was just a foreplay for the real encountering of the other.

Weimer is most pristine in the fall. Streets are colored with vermilion leaves if you have the courage to walk outside the main town. Some inner thrill drives me out to Friesstresse, looking for my way to Ilm. An elderly couple responds to my Guten Morgen with their regular voice of ‘warum bist du hier?’

This, to me, is a philosophical question. And I was there for my PhD in philosophy. I have always believed that university is an open space and we can learn from all around. I considered that couple as my teachers and decided to meditate that day on the very question of ‘warum bin ich hier?’ Translating from classical Sanskrit is not easy, but finding an answer to this question, I soon learnt, was beyond my abilities.

All I could see was the naked other; the other a bit scared, the other incapable of defining his own space. I decided to not apply for an extension of my scholarship.

Returning to Nepal was yet another revelation. The me that existed once was no more. The other me was a scary figure, making orthodox pandits and my monk friends uncomfortable. I knew there was no space for me, but I could not find space for the other me, also.

Haunted in the lands of the others, the me that once existed is no more. I work for the other me. Buried deep, the me makes an effort to have some voice of his own. I shout at him: warum bist du hier?

The Philosopher’s Stone

A man sets out on a journey in search of the Philosopher’s Stone, the Cintamani. By a stroke of extraordinary luck, he finds it almost immediately, with hardly any effort. Yet being a highly rational man, he begins to doubt his discovery.

“A true Cintamani could not possibly be found so easily,” he reasons. “This must be a fake.”

Convinced by his logic, he throws the stone away and continues his quest for the real one.

He is still searching.
